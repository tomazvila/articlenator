# Grease The Groove Uses Frequent Submaximal Sets For Neural Adaptation

Grease the Groove is a method of executing a single high-quality set of a skill several times throughout the day at roughly half of maximum capacity, accumulating neural exposure without fatigue, to rapidly build coordination and motor patterns.

## Why This Matters

This is the highest-leverage tool for coordination-dependent skills because it exploits the nervous system's ability to learn through frequent, perfect repetitions without the fatigue that degrades form in traditional sessions.

## Details
- In a conventional session, only the first two or three consecutive attempts of a skill are truly productive; subsequent attempts exhaust the nervous system without adding further value.
- Instead of grinding one long session, the trainee takes roughly half of their current maximum reps or hold time and performs several easy submaximal sets spaced hours apart throughout the day.
- The entire goal is to never build up fatigue, keeping the nervous system fresh for pristine form and activation on every rep.
- It is especially effective for coordination and balance skills like the handstand and one-arm handstand.
- The method mirrors how a baby learns to walk: attempting the movement dozens of times a day at low intensity until coordination clicks.
- A practical implementation is to spread attempts with thirty to sixty minutes of rest between them throughout the day, allowing ten or more productive attempts per day for a single skill, with each attempt feeling fresh rather than progressively degraded.
- Because the body stays warmed up throughout the day after the initial warm-up, subsequent attempts require only a brief five-minute warm-up rather than a full session warm-up.
- The method can be combined with the alternating push-pull technique: one planche attempt, one hour rest, one front lever attempt, one hour rest, cycling through the day.
- On workdays, the method can be applied by doing a few attempts before work (not at full effort), continuing after work, and doing another part in the evening—spreading the total attempts across the whole day rather than compressing them into one session.
- GtG sets should be kept at 40 to 60 percent of maximum capacity—if the trainee can do five reps, they should do two to three reps per set, never more—to keep sets fresh and high quality.
- For strength skills, GtG sets must be submaximal but still heavy enough to recruit high-threshold motor units; lifting a light load (like a pen) recruits only small motor units, while heavy loads force the nervous system to recruit the larger motor units that produce the most force.
- GtG should be mixed with at least one or two regular structured training sessions per week to maintain clear progressive overload tracking, because GtG alone can become messy with unclear volume, reps, and RPE.
- Grease the Groove does not replace a training session — it is an addition to it. Using only GTG without regular structured sessions may produce initial progress but will not sustain it long term. GTG supplements training by spreading sets throughout the day to reduce overall fatigue and add weekly volume without hurting recovery.
- A weekly schedule combining GtG with regular sessions might use normal training on Tuesday and Saturday with GtG on Monday and Thursday.
- When time is limited, resting eight to fifteen minutes between sets in a single session produces similar effects to spreading attempts across the day.
- GtG should not be used for movements that are unsafe without a proper warm-up, such as the maltese, iron cross, or hefesto, because doing these cold throughout the day risks injury.
- The Grease the Groove method can be applied specifically to planche training by spreading ten attempts throughout the day instead of doing five consecutive attempts that degrade in quality; this method should be used one to two times per week, or daily for trainees who work from home and can fit attempts between work tasks.
- The Grease the Groove method can be applied to planche by doing all skill attempts throughout the day with a quick five-minute warm-up every thirty minutes to one hour, spreading ten attempts across seven to eight hours so that every attempt feels fresh rather than degraded by accumulated fatigue.
- Greasing the groove can produce the most rapid front lever progress, using a protocol of five sessions per week mixing small EMOM sessions (5-10 sets of 5-10 minute EMOM) spaced throughout the day with one weighted session per week.

## Grounded Example
A trainee who can hold a freestanding handstand for a maximum of 15 seconds does 7-second handstand holds every hour throughout the day, 8-10 times total. Over two weeks, the frequent neural exposure builds the motor pattern so effectively that his max hold time jumps to 30 seconds without any strength training.
