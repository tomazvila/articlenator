# Active Recovery On Rest Days Improves Next Workout Performance

Replacing full rest days with light activity—such as running, swimming, or submaximal sets of lower progressions at ten to twenty percent of maximum effort—keeps the body activated and produces better results in the following workout than complete inactivity.

## Why This Matters

Complete rest days leave the body in a dormant state, so the next training session requires a longer warm-up and the nervous system is less responsive; active recovery maintains neural activation and blood flow without adding fatigue.

## Grounded Example

A trainee who rests completely on Saturday does a planche session on Sunday and feels sluggish for the first three attempts. The following week, he replaces Saturday's full rest with a twenty-minute jog and ten submaximal handstand holds at twenty percent effort. On Sunday, his first planche attempt matches his previous best.

## Details

- Active recovery can include running, swimming, dips, pull-ups, push-ups, or lower progressions of the target skills—but only one or two light sets, never approaching failure.
- The intensity should be ten to twenty percent of maximum effort, ensuring the trainee is one hundred percent sure they are not taxing themselves.
- The goal is to keep the body activated so the next training day does not start from a cold dormant state.
- This applies to two of three rest days in a four-workout-per-week schedule.
- Active recovery for calisthenics athletes should use skill-specific [[Grease The Groove Uses Frequent Submaximal Sets For Neural Adaptation|GTG (greasing the groove)]] rather than generic exercises like pull-ups or dips. Since the athlete's goal is planche and front lever, active recovery should consist of light planche and front lever GTG sets — not generic movements irrelevant to the target skills. However, this should only be done if recovery levels are not deteriorating; if recovery is compromised, full rest is better.
