# Proper Planche Form Requires Coordinated Full-Body Engagement Throughout All Progressions

Every planche progression from the lean hold to the straddle requires the simultaneous engagement of pressed-together legs, squeezed glutes, braced core, depressed chest, and squeezed lats as a single coordinated tension pattern.

## Why This Matters

Partial engagement—activating only the core or only the shoulders—creates weak points in the kinetic chain that cause the body to sag or collapse, preventing progress and increasing injury risk.

## Details

- The legs must be pressed together throughout the hold, which facilitates better glute engagement.
- Squeezing the glutes and engaging the core must happen simultaneously and be maintained for the entire hold duration.
- The lats must be squeezed to stabilize the shoulders, and the chest pushed down to maintain protraction.
- The core must be squeezed at maximum intensity throughout the entire hold; a relaxed core makes the planche impossible regardless of upper-body strength and increases the risk of elbow or wrist injury from the body collapsing into a compromised position.
- A relaxed core during the planche causes the body to collapse forward and downward, which transfers excessive load to the elbows and wrists and can cause injury even if the trainee has sufficient shoulder strength.
- In the tuck planche and all subsequent progressions, the biceps must be rotated forward (elbow creases facing forward) to maintain proper shoulder positioning and lat engagement.
- The head must remain neutral (not looking forward or down) to keep the spine in alignment.
- This full-body tension pattern is the same across all progressions; only the leverage changes.
- A common mistake is squeezing the lower back before the glutes and core, which relaxes the core and makes the position impossible to hold.
- In the advanced tuck planche specifically, trainees often squeeze the lower back to try to open the angle between the core and thighs, but this relaxes the core and makes the position impossible to hold; the correct approach is to keep the core squeezed and maintain the full-body tension pattern.
- The biceps must be rotated forward (elbow creases facing forward) throughout all progressions, and the mental cue of squeezing the lats together as if they could touch in the middle of the back helps maintain lat engagement even though that is anatomically impossible.
- Even with perfect upper-body engagement, the planche will fail if the legs are not actively squeezed and connected to the upper body through the glutes, thighs, and lower back; the legs must be treated as an active part of the tension chain, not passive appendages.
- Pressing the legs together during planche holds is not just a passive position but an active engagement that shifts focus from the upper body to the full kinetic chain; many trainees neglect the lower body entirely, which makes the legs feel heavy and difficult to lift, whereas pressing them together engages the glutes and core and makes the legs feel lighter and more controllable.
- In the advanced tuck planche specifically, the trainee must push down and forward simultaneously to maximize the distance between the ground and the knees; pushing only forward causes the body to drop, while pushing only down prevents the forward lean needed for the planche line.
- The wrong hip activation (lack of posterior pelvic tilt) becomes a problem specifically when transitioning from tuck planche to advanced tuck planche — in the tuck position the knees are already close to the body so the mistake is not possible, but extending the legs without squeezing the glutes and core first causes the hips to sag and protraction to be lost, making the entire position impossible to hold.
- The legs must be actively squeezed before they are lifted, not after; attempting to lift relaxed legs opens the core and breaks the tension chain, making the legs feel heavy and uncontrollable regardless of upper-body strength.
- Pressing the knees together in the advanced tuck planche is a practical cue that automatically squeezes the thighs and glutes, giving the trainee much better control over leg positioning during the transition to harder progressions.
- During the straddle planche push-up specifically, the core and chest must remain pushed throughout the entire movement — opening the chest at the bottom position kills the momentum needed for the concentric push and makes the push-up impossible regardless of upper-body strength.
- The triceps should touch the lats at the bottom of the straddle planche push-up descent, using the lats to support the body weight and create a stable platform from which to push back up.
- The lower back must be actively pushed down during the straddle planche push-up descent while the glutes remain squeezed; failing to push the lower back down causes the hips to sag and the planche line to break, which makes the concentric push impossible.

## Grounded Example

A trainee attempting the advanced tuck planche engages their core but forgets to squeeze their glutes and press their legs together. Their hips sag below shoulder height and they cannot hold for more than three seconds. After learning to engage everything simultaneously, the same trainee holds a clean advanced tuck for eight seconds with the hips at shoulder height.
